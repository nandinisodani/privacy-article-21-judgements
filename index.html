From privacy as liberty to informational self-determination: Article 21 in the Supreme Court, 2021–2026

1. Where the decisions fall

The early cases (2021–22) are about the State's reach: spyware (Manohar Lal Sharma), the body (Inayath Ali) and reproductive choice (X v. Principal Secretary). Informational privacy keeps recurring after Pegasus, through the Electoral Bonds case, location-tracking as a bail condition and secret call recordings. Compelled DNA testing returns in 2022, 2023, 2025 and 2026 and is the longest line of cases in the dataset.

2. Did the privacy claim win?

Phase 1 · 2021–2023: privacy as a trump. All four judgments (Pegasus, Inayath Ali, X, Aparna Firodia) go the way of the privacy claim. Puttaswamy supplies the test (legality, legitimate aim, proportionality) and the State has to justify intrusion.
Phase 2 · 2024: proportionality bites in both directions. In Electoral Bonds informational privacy is accepted as real but cannot justify total donor anonymity. Frank Vitus strikes down location-tracking bail conditions. The Court is applying the test, not just invoking the right.
Phase 3 · 2025–2026: privacy as one interest among others. Vibhor Garg holds spousal privacy is "not absolute" and gives way to the evidence rule, and Chaturbhuj Pradhan orders a DNA test because the son's interest in his parentage outweighs the father's privacy. The earlier DNA cases (Ivan Rathinam, R. Rajendran) still protect privacy, but they now speak of a "balance of interests".

All three outcomes where privacy yielded fall in 2024 or later. With n = 11 this is a pattern to test, not a trend to assert.

3. Who litigates, and who wins

This is mainly a body-and-family-law line of cases. The party who brought the case won in ten of eleven; the exception is the 2026 paternity appeal, where the appellant lost to the other individual. The one institutional claim (electoral bonds) was an NGO challenging the State, and it won because of voters' information rights, not donors' privacy.

4. From "liberty" to "informational self-determination"?

Reading the issue and holding lines side by side, the Court's language stays in the vocabulary of Puttaswamy: dignity, autonomy, bodily integrity and proportionality. In the passages we read, no judgment adopts "informational self-determination" as a named doctrine. The closest are Pegasus, Electoral Bonds and Frank Vitus, where the Court treats control over information about one's movements, associations and communications as part of privacy. The evolution is therefore best described as privacy widening from liberty and autonomy (bodily and reproductive choice) to data-like interests, while becoming more conditional once it competes with another right. It is not a clean shift to a self-determination model.

6. Method and limits

Judgments were identified from the Supreme Court Observer, law reports and official Supreme Court PDFs, within the window and with reasoned decisions only. Interim orders and pending matters (e.g. the digital-device search guidelines case) were excluded. Eleven judgments are not the whole of the Court's privacy case law, so the percentages describe this sample only. The "dimension" and "privacy prevailed/yielded" tags are our own analytic coding, derived from the issue and holding columns.
